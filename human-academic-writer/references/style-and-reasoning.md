# Style and reasoning

## Think before drafting

For each paragraph, determine internally:

1. Its primary claim or purpose.
2. The evidence, example, or reasoning that supports it.
3. Why the point matters.
4. Its logical relationship to surrounding paragraphs.
5. What the reader must understand before continuing.

Usually develop one main idea per paragraph. Use `claim -> explanation -> evidence/example -> implication` when it suits the material, but do not force it.

## Natural academic prose

Write like a competent researcher carefully explaining the work:

- Mix short, medium, and occasional longer analytical sentences.
- Let paragraph length follow the complexity of the idea.
- Prefer precise statements to praise, decoration, and vague academic filler.
- Use transitions only when they express a real logical relationship.
- Keep grammar correct; naturalness comes from intellectual variation, not deliberate errors.
- Use discipline-appropriate vocabulary and accurate technical terminology.

Avoid habitual or unnecessary use of stock phrases such as *It is important to note that*, *It is worth mentioning that*, *In today's rapidly evolving world*, *This study aims to shed light on*, *delve into*, *a testament to*, *pivotal*, *multifaceted*, *landscape*, *realm*, *underscores*, *plays a crucial role*, and *In conclusion*.

Also avoid:

- Generic openings and artificial paragraph transitions.
- Repetitive sentence openings, paragraph templates, and conclusions.
- Uniform paragraph lengths or grammatically symmetrical sentences throughout.
- Excess transition words, adjectives, em dashes, and repeated three-item lists.
- Inflated vocabulary, obvious restatement, and overexplaining simple concepts.
- Repeating the research objective across multiple sections.

Do not synonym-swap mechanically. Restructure sentences and paragraphs when that improves the logic. Favor concrete mechanisms and effects: explain what a method changes, how it operates, or which trade-off it creates rather than calling it powerful, remarkable, or effective.

## Claim calibration

Choose verbs that reflect evidential strength: *shows*, *suggests*, *indicates*, *supports*, or *demonstrates*. Do not use *proves* unless the design supports proof. Do not claim statistical significance without a suitable analysis.
